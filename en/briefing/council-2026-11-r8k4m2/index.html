The administration of the Municipality of Ormstown, August 2024 to October 2026

What was said, what the rules require, and what the public record shows. A file to guide council's own verification.

Prepared for the members of council by Jesse Roskies, The Ormstown Observer · November 22, 2026 · Working document for council · unlisted page, not for general circulation

Read this first. The author is a party to litigation with the Municipality (Superior Court file 760-17-007472-254), in which the director general is the Municipality's sworn witness. That is a conflict council should weigh. For that reason this file draws no conclusion about any person: it sets the record beside the rules and lists what council can check in documents the public cannot see. Nothing here comes from the court file. “No resolution found” means none was found in the published minutes; it does not mean none exists. Many decisions below were voted by council itself, and the file says so where it applies. Legal references are pointers for the Municipality's advisers, not legal opinions.

3What council actually approves each month

Each month council approves a list of payments (Annexe A of the minutes). The list has up to three blocks: bills to pay, salaries, and payments already issued during the month. The chart compares the amount written in the resolution with the total of the list attached to it.

Difference over the seven sittings: $3,512,643.36. Sources: Res. 25-04-126, Res. 25-08-232, Res. 25-09-252, Res. 25-11-304, Res. 26-06-150, Res. 26-07-187, Res. 26-10-268.

In April, September and November 2025 the resolution quotes one block only. In August 2025 the list prints no grand total and the resolution approves only the bills to pay. In June and July 2026 the resolution quotes the block of payments already made. In October 2026 it quotes the bills to pay; salaries, payments issued and credit-line invoices are on the list but not in the figure.

Two lists do not add up. Sept. 9, 2024: the printed lines of the bills-to-pay block total $289,814.28 and its printed subtotal is $291,514.28 ($1,700.00 apart). July 14, 2025: the three printed subtotals total $1,828,963.31 and the grand total adopted is $1,818,914.95 ($10,048.36 apart).

What council can ask: that each resolution state the grand total and each block; that the block of payments already issued show who authorized each one and under which power; and an explanation of the two arithmetic gaps from the accounting system.

7Staff turnover and its cost

Hiring and managing staff is the director general's field: the mayor told residents on April 13, 2026 that personnel management belongs to the administration and that he may not step into an employee's file (▶ video, 2:03:00 · Observer copy). The chart is built from the hiring and vacancy dates written in council's resolutions.

The clerk's post. Vacant since January 2024 when the DG arrived; filled Aug. 5, 2024 (res. 24-08-219); new clerk named March 13, 2025 (res. 25-03-104); vacant from June 12; filled Sept. 15, 2025 (res. 25-09-256); vacant from May 7, 2026; filled Sept. 9, 2026 (res. 26-08-220). During each vacancy the DG acts as clerk-treasurer, as the Code municipal provides (art. 184).

Accounting. A technician hired Aug. 5, 2024 (res. 24-08-220) had her contract ended as of Aug. 30 (res. 24-09-234), with no reason given. A second was hired Dec. 2, 2024 (res. 24-12-318) and a third March 13, 2025 (res. 25-03-105), through a recruitment firm. The public works director's post was vacant from July 10 to Sept. 17, 2026 (res. 26-07-192, res. 26-09-261).

Departures settled by agreement. res. 24-09-249: settlement of a complaint over a dismissal made in March 2024, before the DG. Res. 25-03-102: a post abolished on Feb. 14, 2025 in an “administrative reorganization”, with an agreement signed the same day. Res. 25-03-103: the Town told an employee on March 6, 2025 that it wished to end her contract; agreement the next day. None states an amount. All three were ratified by council.

Outside HR and recruitment support: $134,854.95

What council can ask: the cost of the three agreements; the reorganization plan of early 2025 and who approved it; exit-interview notes for the clerks; the list of people hired under delegated power, which the Code municipal requires to be tabled (art. 165.1); the replacement guarantees in the recruitment contracts.

Paid to BCGO, Aug. 2024 to Oct. 2026: $155,689.06, of which $44,048.30 for support, training and preparation work.

BCGO is the Municipality's external auditor. It was also paid for work that is not auditing: budget support at $160 an hour with no ceiling (res. 24-08-216, the DG's first regular sitting), “preparing the 2024 audit” ($16,671.38), training, and support under res. 25-08-237. Outside legal fees by quarter, from the payment lists. Q3 2024 covers August and September only; Q4 2026 covers October only.

Outside legal fees came to $447,886.55 over the 27 lists. Dunton Rainville received $323,397 (72%). 11Interests and independence

One name on a contract and on a committeeThe Aedifica question in section 4. If the two are one person, the question is whether an interest was declared before the committee or council dealt with anything touching the firm, and who in the administration knew.
The auditor as adviserSection 8. An auditor checks the administration's work on council's behalf; the more it helps prepare that work, the less distance it has.
One law firm, several rolesSection 9. Litigation counsel, election president and stand-in clerk are three different positions toward council and the public. On Oct. 6, 2026 residents were told access-to-information work had been delegated to an outside firm until late September (▶ video, 14:50 · Observer copy; speaker to confirm).
One officer, three functionsFor about 7 of the 27 months the clerk's post was vacant and the DG also acted as clerk. The law allows it. It removes the second pair of eyes on the minutes, the payment list and access requests, which is when council's own checks matter most.
What the public cannot seeEmployees' declarations of conflict are internal. Only council can ask whether the DG has ever referred an appearance of conflict to the mayor under s. 7.4 of the code, and whether the yearly attestations exist.

12What the review did not find

  • No document links the DG personally to any supplier. The review looked for shared names, addresses and prior employers in the public record and found none.
  • His own expenses are small. Lines that name the DG (laptop, phone, office set-up, memberships, training, a conference, his card) total about $16,000 over 27 months. Three 2025 card statements appear with no detail ($5,501.24); since October 2025 card lines are itemized, which is an improvement. One itemized line, “Rest. ZIBO – Formation DG”, $1,192.04, is worth a question on purpose and attendees.
  • Several flagged contracts predate him. Sismik was contracted in May 2024 and the communications mandate was voted on July 8, 2024; he was named on July 15 and started on July 22.
  • Most large contracts went to public tender and were awarded to the lowest bidder by council: 11 of the 16 on the Observer's contracts register.
  • Mandatory filings were made where the green marks appear in sections 2 and 10.

13What council can ask for

  1. For every item in sections 4 to 6: the contract or purchase order, the quotes obtained, the invoices, and the written authorization for any change.
  2. A list of every purchase the DG approved under his delegated power since July 2024, with date, supplier and amount, and from now on the report the Code municipal requires at each regular sitting.
  3. What became of by-law 130-2024, and which delegation by-law the administration has applied since August 2024.
  4. The rotation forms for every negotiated contract of $25,000 or more, and the yearly contractor lists for 2023, 2024 and 2025.
  5. The auditor's management letters and independence confirmations for 2024 and 2025.
  6. The DG's contract as updated in March 2025, and council's written evaluations.
  7. The cost of the three departure agreements and the 2025 reorganization plan.
  8. The legal-mandates table described in section 9.
  9. The deliverables in section 6, in full, to council.

If council wants an independent look: the Commission municipale du Québec audits municipalities and receives disclosures of wrongdoing; the Autorité des marchés publics examines how contracts were awarded; the ministère des Affaires municipales et de l'Habitation can verify a municipality's administration. Council can also mandate its own external auditor for a special examination under a resolution that sets the questions.
