Wind turbines in the Haut-Saint-Laurent: where things actually stand

A Hydro-Québec map, a petition, lawn signs and a survey in every mailbox have put wind energy on the region’s agenda. The Observer read the laws, the bylaw, the tender documents and the public statements. This is what the record shows, and what it doesn’t.

The Ormstown Observer · November 16, 2026 · Haut-Saint-Laurent

Where things stand on November 16, 2026

  • No wind project has been proposed in the MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent. The MRC says none is planned or under review (MRC).
  • The “1,000 megawatts” figure is Hydro-Québec’s estimate of how much wind power its grid could take in. It is not a project, a site or a number of turbines.
  • The MRC’s 2006 wind bylaw is still in force: a commercial turbine must stand at least 2 km from any home and 1 km from any public road. The MRC says that while it is in force, no wind project can be established on its territory (MRC).
  • The MRC is rewriting its rules, as Québec now requires of every MRC. Survey answers are collected November 3 to 22; a proposed regulatory framework is expected in January 2027, with written briefs in February and a public consultation in March. The MRC calls these dates approximate (MRC).
  • Hydro-Québec’s call for tenders requires a formal resolution of support from each local body where a project would be located (tender document).

Over the past year, wind energy has become one of the most talked-about subjects in the Haut-Saint-Laurent. Lawn signs have gone up, a petition has circulated online, residents have spoken at consultations in two MRCs, and every household in the region is receiving a survey card in the mail.

Much of the conversation has run ahead of the documents. This article sets out what has actually happened, what would be required before a wind farm could be built here, and where each of those steps stands today. Wherever we can, we link to the law, the bylaw or the official document itself, and quote its exact wording. Where we rely on another publication’s reporting, we say so. Where something isn’t known, we say that too.

Where it started: a map of the grid

On December 11, 2025, Hydro-Québec published a map of the integration capacity of its transmission grid: roughly how much new wind generation each part of the grid could take in for start-up between 2031 and 2035 (Hydro-Québec presentation). One electrical sector, labelled “Huntington – Châteauguay,” was given up to 1,000 megawatts (MW), tied with Nicolet for the largest figure on the map (map). A neighbouring sector, “Coteau-du-Lac – Valleyfield,” was given up to 300 MW.

Hydro-Québec’s presentation is explicit about what the map is. Only technical grid factors were considered. The figures are preliminary and may change. Developers remain responsible for social and environmental acceptability, and only projects backed by the municipalities concerned will proceed (presentation).

The map does not say where turbines would go, and it does not measure the wind. That job falls to developers: the call for tenders requires every bid to include an expert report describing the site’s energy potential (tender document, art. 2.2.1 (I)). The farmers’ union, the UPA, estimated that 1,000 MW could mean up to about 145 turbines, The Gleaner reported. That is the UPA’s estimate, not Hydro-Québec’s (The Gleaner).

What “Huntingdon–Châteauguay” means, and doesn’t

The name refers to a part of Hydro-Québec’s electrical grid, which Hydro-Québec’s own documents spell “Huntington.” Hydro-Québec notes that the names of its electrical sectors may differ from the usual names of administrative regions (presentation). It is not a municipal boundary or a defined strip of land.

The call for tenders lists the grid connection points that are eligible in the sector for its first round: the De Léry substation and lines L1213-L1214 and L1566, at 120 kV; lines L3086-L3087, at 315 kV; and a 60 MW expansion of the existing Parc éolien de la Montérégie. A single wind farm within 5 km of lines L3086-L3087 could connect up to 1,000 MW, falling to 600 MW at 40 km. Hydro-Québec says these figures are indicative only (tender document, art. 2.2.2.3 and Annex 4). The documents do not say where those lines run or which municipalities they cross.

Who would build a wind farm, and how

Hydro-Québec now uses two models. Very large projects, which can exceed 1,000 MW, are led by Hydro-Québec with partners. Smaller projects, under 350 MW, are proposed by private developers through calls for tenders (Hydro-Québec presentation). The Huntington – Châteauguay sector is one of the areas open in the first round of the current call, A/O 2026-01 (tender document, Annex 4).

That call was launched on April 10, 2026. Across all of Québec, it seeks 5 to 10 terawatt-hours of new energy a year, to be awarded in up to three rounds; how much is actually bought depends on the bids received (tender document, introduction). Hydro-Québec puts that at about 1,500 to 3,000 MW of installed capacity (Hydro-Québec). The tender document sets these conditions, among others (tender document):

  • Local support. Each bid must show that « chaque Milieu local, tel que défini au Contrat-type, où est situé le projet de parc éolien appui formellement ledit projet » (Observer translation: “each local community, as defined in the model contract, where the wind farm project is located formally supports the project”), by a certified copy of a signed resolution (art. 2.2.1 (I)). The model contract defines that local community as one or more bodies, including « une MRC » and « une municipalité locale » (model contract, art. 1.1). A bid may carry a support “in principle,” but a formal resolution is required before the contract is signed, and a resolution that grants a developer exclusivity on a territory leads to the bid’s rejection.
  • Public consultation must take place before a bid is submitted, and developers must show how the concerns raised were taken into account (Hydro-Québec).
  • Payments to the community. Each local community that administers the territory must receive « un montant annuel de 6 227 $ par MW installé » (“an annual amount of $6,227 per MW installed”), indexed (art. 2.2.1 (J)).
  • Land. Where the project uses private land, the bidder must hold « des lettres d’intention ou des contrats d’octroi d’option valides pour au moins 50 % des unités d’évaluation » on which its infrastructure would sit (“valid letters of intent or option contracts for at least 50% of the assessment units”). Options signed with a private owner after October 20, 2026 cannot last more than 60 months (art. 2.2.2.5.5). The tender documents contain no expropriation mechanism.
  • Local rules. The bidder must demonstrate « la conformité du site avec les lois et règlements applicables en matière d’aménagement et d’urbanisme », a list that names the MRC’s land-use plan, its interim control bylaw (RCI) and municipal zoning (art. 2.2.2.5.5).
  • Farmland. Hydro-Québec’s framework for wind farms on farm and forest land, revised with the UPA, sets compensation for landowners, including an annual payment of at least $3,000 per megawatt installed on their property (framework, s. 5.2.6.1). Following it is optional unless the bidder commits to it in its bid: Hydro-Québec writes that « l’utilisation du Cadre de référence demeure toutefois facultative » (Q&A, Q-0005; tender document, art. 2.2.2.5.3).
  • Ranking. Bids are ranked on the proposed price of electricity, adjusted for qualitative criteria (Stikeman Elliott) including Québec content and limiting the use of the best farmland (Hydro-Québec). Hydro-Québec has also confirmed support for a UPA fund for agriculture (Hydro-Québec); The Gleaner reported the contribution at up to $12,500 per megawatt commissioned in agricultural zones (The Gleaner).

A project that wins a contract still needs separate authorizations. In a designated agricultural region, « une personne ne peut, sans l’autorisation de la commission, utiliser un lot à une fin autre que l’agriculture » (“a person may not, without the authorization of the commission, use a lot for any purpose other than agriculture”) (Act respecting the preservation of agricultural land, s. 26). And « un parc éolien […] d’une puissance égale ou supérieure à 10 MW » is subject to Québec’s environmental impact assessment procedure, which includes the BAPE (regulation, Schedule 1, Part II, s. 11).

The first-round deadline for bids is February 26, 2027, for projects starting up between 2031 and 2035. The tender document schedules a second round with bids due August 11, 2028, and a third with bids due February 25, 2030, dates it labels « à titre indicatif » (indicative) and subject to change by addendum (tender document, s. 3.1).

Who decides? Where the power lies

  • Landowners can say no to a private developer. Bidders must hold signed letters of intent or option contracts, and the tender documents contain no expropriation mechanism (tender document). Hydro-Québec itself is different: the law lets it expropriate property needed to produce or transmit electricity, with the government’s authorization (Hydro-Québec Act, s. 33).
  • The local council, the MRC, or both must pass a formal resolution before a project in the current call for tenders can be signed. The tender’s “local community” can be an MRC or a local municipality, among others, and the documents do not say which one, or whether both, would be needed here (tender document). That is a Hydro-Québec tender rule, not a law, so it could change in later rounds.
  • The MRC decides where turbines may go and how far they must stand from homes, roads and rivers, through its land-use plan and its interim bylaw. Municipal bylaws must be « au moins aussi contraignantes » (at least as restrictive) (Land Use Planning Act, s. 6), and a bid must show it conforms to the MRC’s bylaw (tender document).
  • The Québec government sets the orientations every MRC must follow, including one to « favoriser la mise en valeur du potentiel éolien du territoire » (promote the territory’s wind potential) (orientations), and its ministries and agencies review the MRC’s new rules before they take effect (MRC). Next door, the MRC de Beauharnois-Salaberry tells its municipalities they may adopt stricter rules but must still allow turbines in the zones the MRC has found compatible (MRC de Beauharnois-Salaberry).
  • Hydro-Québec buys the power and writes the tender rules. If it builds a wind farm itself, the law requires the government’s authorization (Hydro-Québec Act, s. 29). That brings the project under a separate procedure for government work: the MRC gives an opinion on whether it conforms; if it does not, the minister can ask the MRC to amend its plan; and if the MRC does not, the government can adopt the amendment itself by decree, after a public consultation (Land Use Planning Act, ss. 149–157). The documents we reviewed do not say whether a project in which Hydro-Québec is only a partner would count.
  • The CPTAQ and Québec’s environment ministry must authorize a project separately, and can refuse it (s. 26; regulation).
  • Ottawa has no say over where wind turbines go. That is the difference with cell towers: in 2016, in a case from Châteauguay, the Supreme Court ruled that a municipality cannot block an antenna at a site the federal government has approved (Rogers v. Châteauguay).

The legal text behind each decision

The box above is a summary. These are the exact words it rests on. Québec’s laws are quoted in French, the version that prevails; the English is the Observer’s translation.

  • Municipal rules must follow the MRC. The MRC’s land-use plan contains a document that sets rules for municipal bylaws, « notamment quant au fait qu’un tel règlement doit être adopté et doit contenir des dispositions au moins aussi contraignantes que celles prévues au document complémentaire » (“in particular that such a bylaw must be adopted and must contain provisions at least as restrictive as those in the complementary document”) (Land Use Planning Act, s. 6).
  • Interim control. While it revises its plan, an MRC may « interdire les nouvelles utilisations du sol, les nouvelles constructions » (“prohibit new land uses, new constructions”) (s. 62; by bylaw, s. 64). No municipal permit may be issued for work the interim rules forbid (s. 68). That is the legal basis of RCI 223-2006.
  • The provincial orientation. Orientation 9: « Favoriser la mise en valeur du potentiel éolien du territoire d’une manière qui respecte les particularités du milieu et qui contribue à l’acceptabilité sociale de cette filière énergétique » (“Promote the territory’s wind potential in a way that respects local characteristics and contributes to the social acceptance of this energy sector”). Objective 9.1 adds: « prendre en compte les caractéristiques distinctives du milieu et les préoccupations de la population » (“take into account the area’s distinctive characteristics and the population’s concerns”) (orientations).
  • When Hydro-Québec builds. « la construction d’un immeuble destiné à la production d’électricité par la Société doit être autorisée par le gouvernement » (“the construction of an immovable intended for electricity production by the Corporation must be authorized by the government”) (Hydro-Québec Act, s. 29). The procedure for government work excludes most Hydro-Québec work, but not « une construction devant, en vertu de la Loi sur Hydro-Québec (chapitre H-5), être autorisée au préalable par le gouvernement » (“a construction that must, under the Hydro-Québec Act, be authorized beforehand by the government”) (Land Use Planning Act, s. 149).
  • What that procedure allows. The MRC must give « son avis sur la conformité de l’intervention projetée » (its opinion on the conformity of the planned work) within 120 days (s. 152). If the MRC fails to amend its plan when asked, « le gouvernement peut se substituer au conseil » (“the government may take the council’s place”) and, after a public consultation, adopt the amendment by decree, which « est réputé adopté par le conseil » (“is deemed adopted by the council”) (s. 156).
  • Expropriation. « Avec l’autorisation du gouvernement, la Société peut […] acquérir, par voie d’expropriation […] tous immeubles, servitudes ou constructions requis […] pour la production, la transmission ou la distribution d’énergie » (“With the government’s authorization, the Corporation may acquire by expropriation any immovables, servitudes or constructions required for the production, transmission or distribution of energy”) (Hydro-Québec Act, s. 33). The Act gives this power to Hydro-Québec, not to private developers.

What the rules say here today

Since 2007, wind turbines in the Haut-Saint-Laurent have been governed by the MRC’s interim control bylaw, RCI 223-2006, amended by bylaw 264-2013 in force since March 18, 2013. According to The Gleaner, the amendment followed residents’ rejection of a proposal for 10 turbines in Godmanchester, which was never approved (The Gleaner).

For a commercial turbine, the bylaw requires at least 2,000 metres from any dwelling (art. 24), any campground (art. 26) and the Droulers archaeological site (art. 27.1); 1,000 metres on each side of a public road (art. 23) and of the regional linear park (art. 27.1); and 600 metres on each side of the Châteauguay, Trout and des Anglais rivers (art. 20). No new house may be built within 2,000 metres of a turbine (art. 25), and turbines are limited to uncultivated land (art. 27).

Two readings of the bylaw are on the record. Its text says that, subject to those distances, « l’implantation d’éoliennes commerciales est autorisée à l’intérieur de la zone 3 » (“the installation of commercial wind turbines is authorized within Zone 3”) (art. 19); its map labels zones 1 and 2 « zone d’interdiction aux éoliennes » and Zone 3 « zone favorable aux éoliennes ». The MRC, for its part, answers the question “Are wind turbines allowed on the territory?” this way: « Non. Le règlement de contrôle intérimaire actuellement en vigueur a pour effet de ne permettre aucun projet éolien sur le territoire de la MRC, ce qui contrevient toutefois aux nouvelles OGAT » (“No. The interim control bylaw now in force has the effect of allowing no wind project on the MRC’s territory, which, however, runs counter to the new provincial orientations”) (MRC). The Observer has not mapped whether any land in Zone 3 meets every distance.

Le Nouvelliste ranked the bylaw the strictest of the wind bylaws it compared in October, tied for the largest separation distance and with the highest fines (Le Nouvelliste). One detail for readers checking the record: the MRC’s consultation page calls the bylaw “RCI 220-2006,” while the bylaw document itself is numbered 223-2006 (MRC; bylaw).

Why the rules are being rewritten

On December 1, 2024, new provincial land-use orientations took effect, including Orientation 9 on wind energy quoted above (Gouvernement du Québec). Every MRC must revise its land-use plan to follow them.

The MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent has begun that revision. It says RCI 223-2006 stays in force throughout, that « aucun changement ne sera apporté à la réglementation en vigueur » (“no change will be made to the regulations in force”) before public consultations, and that the separation distances will take residents’ concerns into account (MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent). The Gleaner reported that residents raised wind energy repeatedly at seven land-use consultations in February and March (The Gleaner).

The MRC describes a seven-step process, from analysing the territory to entry into force and municipal concordance (MRC; MRC):

  • Now: analysis of the territory and the household survey. The MRC is updating the zones where turbines would be incompatible, weighing ecological interest, landscapes, agriculture, and health and quality of life.
  • January 2027: a proposed regulatory framework, drafted after the survey results and made public.
  • February 2027: a box opens for written briefs (mémoires).
  • March 2027: a public consultation on the proposal; meetings with territorial partners are also planned for the spring.
  • Summer 2027: the rules go into a first draft of the revised land-use plan (schéma).
  • Then: the plan’s adoption process, with further consultations. The MRC says its regulation « devra passer le test de plusieurs ministères et d’organismes avant d’être adoptée » (“will have to pass the test of several ministries and agencies before being adopted”). They then replace the 2006 bylaw, and « les municipalités comprises dans la MRC devront adopter des normes au moins aussi contraignantes » (“the municipalities in the MRC will have to adopt standards at least as restrictive”).

The MRC writes: « Veuillez noter que les dates sont approximatives » (“Please note that the dates are approximate”) (MRC). Based on that timetable, the MRC’s new rules will not be in force by Hydro-Québec’s first-round deadline of February 26, 2027. Until they are, the 2006 bylaw applies, and the tender requires bids to conform to it.

The survey in your mailbox

The MRC hired the polling firm Léger to survey residents on wind turbines and other renewable energy. Canada Post is delivering a card to each private household, about 10,252 of them, between November 2 and 20, and answers are collected from November 3 to 22, 2026. Each card carries a randomly generated access code that can be used once. The MRC says the codes are tied to the printed cards but not to an address or to anyone in the household, and that the file used to check codes is kept apart from the answers. The questionnaire is offered in French and English; the card shown on the MRC’s website is in French. No date has been given for the results (MRC).

The MRC’s pages on the process are in French. Their link to an English version leads to a general English news page that, when the Observer checked on November 16, did not include them (MRC). People who need help can call the MRC at 450 264-5411 or write to mrchsl@mrchsl.com (MRC).

MRC prefect Yves Métras, the mayor of Franklin, told The Gleaner that residents had asked to receive information directly at home, and that this campaign responds to that request (The Gleaner).

Who has said what

  • The MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent: « Aucun projet éolien n’est actuellement prévu sur le territoire ni analysé par la MRC. Advenant qu’un projet soit proposé, il est assuré que son implantation sera encadrée par la MRC » (“No wind project is currently planned on the territory or under review by the MRC. Should a project be proposed, its siting will be regulated by the MRC”) (MRC). Prefect Yves Métras, in March: « Nous entrons dans cette démarche avec un souci de transparence et de dialogue » (“We are entering this process with a concern for transparency and dialogue”) (MRC).
  • Carole Mallette, then the MNA for Huntingdon, told The Gleaner in May that land-use decisions belong to local councils and that a company such as Hydro-Québec cannot override them. She said the call for tenders is not specific to the region (The Gleaner). For the limits of that, see “Who decides?” above.
  • Hydro-Québec plans to add more than 10,000 MW of wind power by 2035 (Hydro-Québec), and says it will favour projects developed in partnership with host communities and First Nations (Hydro-Québec).
  • The UPA opposes wind farms on the best farmland. The Gleaner reported that its Montérégie federation estimated the region’s mapped capacity at nearly 400 turbines (The Gleaner). In April, the UPA reached an agreement in principle with Hydro-Québec on protecting farmland (Hydro-Québec). Farmers, municipal officials and Hydro-Québec workers have asked Hydro-Québec to suspend the call for tenders (Noovo).
  • Developers have been prospecting in the Montérégie. The Gleaner reported in May that Innergex had met with up to three Haut-Saint-Laurent municipalities and area farmers. The municipalities were not named (The Gleaner).
  • Residents opposed to turbines have put up signs, spoken at the Beauharnois-Salaberry consultation on April 29, The Gleaner reported (The Gleaner), and launched a petition on March 1, 2026, which had 578 signatures when the Observer checked on November 14.

Next door and nearby

Beauharnois-Salaberry has taken a different path. In January 2026, its MRC said Hydro-Québec had identified its territory as suitable for more wind energy and that companies were prospecting (MRC de Beauharnois-Salaberry). It drafted a new framework in March with a minimum of 700 metres between a commercial turbine and a home, held a public consultation on April 29, and adopted a final version on September 16 that increased some distances, to livestock buildings and roads. The final bylaw (334), in force since November 7, 2026, keeps 700 metres as the minimum between a commercial turbine and a building with a “sensitive use,” such as a home (bylaw 334, s. 10.23.3, table 37.1). Meanwhile, no permit for a wind turbine can be issued there until its municipalities bring their own bylaws into line, by May 7, 2027 (MRC de Beauharnois-Salaberry).

Elsewhere in the Montérégie, the Les Jardins wind farm (147 MW, 21 turbines, in the MRC des Jardins-de-Napierville) was selected in Hydro-Québec’s 2023 call for tenders and is in environmental review (MELCCFP registry). The Gleaner reported in February that the CPTAQ had signalled in December 2025 that it could refuse the project to protect farmland (The Gleaner).

How to take part

  • Answer the survey by November 22, using the code on the card mailed to your home.
  • Read the bylaw in force yourself: RCI 223-2006 (PDF, in French).
  • Watch for the proposed framework in January and the consultation dates on the MRC’s wind page.
  • Follow your council’s agendas. A project in the current call for tenders would need a resolution of support before its contract could be signed.
