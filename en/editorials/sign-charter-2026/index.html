A sign, the Charter, and who gets to decide

Ormstown approved a new shop's sign on condition that its English lettering be reworked to comply with the Charter of the French language. The intent is fair. The tool is the wrong one.

By Jesse Roskies, Editor — The Ormstown Observer — November 3, 2026

On November 2, council approved a sign application for Shack Story, a new western-wear shop at 55 rue Lambton (agenda item 9.2, 00:34:11). The colours were accepted. Two conditions came with the approval, both recommended by the planning advisory committee (CCU): the logo has to match the one already on the façade, and the English-only lettering has to be revised to comply with the Charter of the French language (00:34:58–00:36:32). The mayor said the owners had already been told.

The first condition is exactly what a sign review is for. The second is worth a closer look, because it asks a town by-law to do a job it was not written to do.

What Ormstown's own rules say

Signs in the village core go through a PIIA review under Règlement 152-2023. Article 4.6 lists eight criteria for them: the sign should suit the building's architecture, not cover architectural details, sit on a discreet support, be lit in a way that fits the façade, and so on. Not one of the eight mentions language. The town's signage chapter in zoning by-law 148-2023 (Chapter 11) regulates where signs go and how big they are; the Observer found no language rule there either. The CCU may recommend an approval that is conditional on changes (art. 3.7), but its analysis is supposed to rest on the by-law's objectives and criteria.

What the Charter says, and who applies it

English is not banned from Quebec storefronts. Since June 1, 2025, a recognized trademark in another language can appear on outdoor signage, provided French is “markedly predominant,” meaning the French text has a much greater visual impact than the other language (Éducaloi). Whether Shack Story needs French added, and how much, depends on facts that were not discussed in public: whether the name is a registered trademark, and what else is on the storefront. The window already carries a French description of the shop.

Those are judgment calls, and the Charter gives them to the Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF).

What the mayor said

Presenting item 9.2, before anyone had asked a question, the mayor explained the condition (00:34:58–00:35:43):

« …l'enseigne (à poser sur la façade) comporte exclusivement des textes en anglais, ce qui va à l'encontre des dispositions relatives aux affiches prévues par la Charte de la langue française. Quand on donne les permis, on est obligé de faire appliquer la loi et la Charte de la langue française a des conditions d'affichage qu'on doit respecter. »

In English: “…the sign (to be installed on the façade) contains text only in English, which goes against the signage provisions of the Charter of the French language. When we grant permits, we are obliged to enforce the law, and the Charter of the French language has signage conditions that we must respect.”

That is the key misunderstanding. A sign permit and the Charter run on two separate tracks. The permit is a municipal power under Quebec's planning law, and it is decided against the town's own by-laws. The OQLF plays no part in it. Compliance with the Charter's signage rules is the OQLF's job, through its own inspections, orders and, if needed, prosecution. The Charter does not hand that job to municipalities, and Ormstown's by-laws do not give it one.

The Charter does give municipalities language duties, but they cover the town's own language: its own signs, notices and documents (art. 22 and following). Those rules bind the municipality as an organization. A business's storefront is a different matter. The rules for it (art. 58 and 58.1) bind the business, and the OQLF enforces them. The town is responsible for its own signs, not for the language of the shop across the street.

Later, during question period, he was asked whether it is the municipality or the OQLF that enforces the Charter. His answer began: « C'est eux », it's them. He added that when the town authorizes something, it makes sure it complies with laws and regulations, especially on language and signage; that this spares merchants a fine and the cost of redoing a sign; and that the town is there to enforce rules, which he described as regulation « qu'on ne contrôle pas », that the town doesn't control (00:43:34). He also said a French generic word such as “boutique” would probably be enough, and that the inspectors are the specialists (00:41:59–00:42:46).

His point about sparing merchants a fine is fair, and made in good faith. A town that sees a likely problem does a new merchant a favour by saying so before the sign is made.

But the claim at the heart of the decision, that the town is obliged to enforce the Charter when it grants permits, does not hold up.

Is the town obliged? The law says no

  • The Charter names its own enforcers, and towns are not among them. Complaints go to the OQLF, which can inspect, order a business to comply, and refer a file to the Director of Criminal and Penal Prosecutions (DPCP), who decides whether to prosecute (Radio-Canada).
  • The Charter already has a permit sanction, and it belongs to the minister. Under article 204.28, the Minister of the French Language, after consulting the OQLF, may suspend or revoke a business's permit if it breaks the Charter repeatedly, despite OQLF orders and penal proceedings. The minister then notifies the authority that issued the permit. The law expects a town to receive that decision, not to make it, and only after repeated violations, not at a first sign application.
  • A PIIA decision has to rest on the PIIA by-law. Under article 145.19 of the Act respecting land use planning and development (LAU), council approves the plans if they conform to the by-law and disapproves them otherwise. Quebec's own planning guide for municipalities says council must take into account only the criteria in its PIIA by-law. Ormstown's by-law 152-2023 has no language criterion.
  • When Quebec asked towns to help, it asked for speed, not policing. Before the new signage rules took effect on June 1, 2025, the Municipal Affairs ministry (MAMH) asked municipalities to process sign requests diligently, and warned that municipal sign by-laws could get in the way of businesses complying on time (UMQ).
  • Other towns say the same. Brossard said so publicly in 2024 (see below).

None of this stops a town from telling a merchant about the Charter. But the Observer found no provision that requires Ormstown to check a business's sign against the Charter before approving it, and the town has not cited one.

This has happened before

Brossard, 2024: Party Expert

In May 2024, Party Expert, a Quebec-owned party-supply chain in business since 1999, applied for a sign permit for a new store at Quartier Dix30. As in Ormstown, the sign fell under a PIIA review. Brossard's planning committee recommended approval on July 3, but city staff, citing the new language law, referred the owner to the OQLF before issuing the permit. The owner went public, saying the city was holding up her permit over the English word “party” (CTV News).

Brossard answered in a public statement on July 17, 2024. It said it had never refused the sign, and that it does not have the authority to refuse a sign on the basis of language, because that is a matter for the OQLF (Ville de Brossard). The OQLF, for its part, told CTV News that it is not responsible for granting sign permits and does not intervene in that process. Days later, the OQLF concluded that Party Expert's sign complied with the Charter (CTV News).

Montreal, 2025: Pub Burgundy Lion

In August 2025, the OQLF itself told the Burgundy Lion pub that “Burgundy” is an English word and that corrections to its sign were required. The co-owner pushed back: the name refers to the Little Burgundy neighbourhood. On August 14, the OQLF reversed course. It said further analysis showed the sign complied, and called the situation “regrettable” (Global News). CBC reported that the office described its own review as “premature” (CBC).

What the two cases show. In Brossard, a city that only suggested an OQLF check still held a business up, and the sign turned out to comply. Brossard also drew the line that Ormstown did not: it said it had no authority to refuse a sign on language grounds, and the OQLF said permits are not its business. In Montreal, the agency whose job this is still got its first call wrong. If language specialists sometimes need a second look, a town planning committee that reviews colours, materials and lighting is not the place to make that call, least of all as a condition of a permit.

A warning is not a condition

A warning and a permit condition are different things. A warning points the merchant to the agency that decides. A condition turns the town's own reading of the Charter into a requirement for its approval, without a criterion in its own by-law and without the OQLF's expertise. If the town reads the Charter more strictly than the OQLF would, the merchant has nowhere to take that disagreement except back to council.

A better way to do it

  • Keep PIIA review to the PIIA criteria.
  • When a sign may raise a language question, tell the applicant in writing, point them to the OQLF's guidance, and let the OQLF decide.
  • If council believes language belongs in sign review, amend the by-law openly, with a public consultation, so the rule is written down and applies to every storefront the same way.

That last point matters most. Ormstown is a community where French and English share one main street. The rules for that street's language should be written down, consulted on and applied evenly, not settled one sign at a time.

French matters here. So does keeping the decision with the body the law assigns it to. They are the same principle.

Details still to find out
