Finance & Taxation · Draft Bylaw 129-2026

New considerations on how Ormstown handles contracts

Every year the town hires contractors and buys equipment and services with public money. A bylaw sets the rules for how those deals are made. Québec changed the law this spring, and council has tabled a draft to rewrite Ormstown's rules. Here is what the bylaw does, how it has worked, and what would change.

The Ormstown Observer · Bylaws & Regulations Explainer

The short version

  • The bylaw decides how the town picks who gets a contract: when it can simply hire a supplier, when it must compare prices, and when it must open the job to bids.
  • Under the current rules, the town can hire a supplier directly, without competing offers, for jobs worth from $25,000 up to the provincial tender threshold. The draft would end that. From $25,000, at least two businesses would have to be invited to bid.
  • For smaller jobs, from $5,000 to $25,000, the town would need at least two prices and would pick the lowest.
  • Fines for lying on a bid, padding an invoice or trying to sway the people choosing a winner would rise from $1,000–$2,000 today to as much as $100,000.
  • It is still a draft. Council votes on it at a later sitting, and residents can ask questions before then.

What this bylaw is for

When Ormstown paves a street, buys a truck, hires an engineer or orders road salt, it is spending public money. Every Québec municipality must have a bylaw on gestion contractuelle, or contract management. The bylaw sets out how it chooses who gets that work.

The rules have two goals. The first is fairness: local businesses should get a fair shot at town work, and no one should get a contract because of who they know. The second is value: the town should get a good price and good work for taxpayers. To get there, the bylaw sets out:

  • how to choose a supplier, depending on how much the job is worth;
  • integrity rules against bid-rigging, bribes, undisclosed lobbying and conflicts of interest;
  • who can approve changes to a contract once it is signed, and how much they can approve;
  • penalties when someone breaks the rules.

How it has worked until now

Ormstown's current rules are Bylaw 129-2020, in force since March 2020, and a 2021 amendment, Bylaw 129-2021. In plain terms:

  • The bigger the job, the more competition. The largest contracts must go out to public tender: anyone can bid, through Québec's online tendering system, SEAO. Below that, the law called for invitations to bid in some cases.
  • But the town could also skip competition in a wide middle range. For contracts from $25,000 up to the threshold for a public tender, the 2020 bylaw allowed a direct award (de gré à gré). The town negotiates with one supplier instead of comparing offers. In return, it was supposed to rotate among suppliers where it could, so the same business didn't always get the work.
  • Below $25,000, the bylaw set no method at all.
  • Bidders sign a sworn statement (solemn declaration). It says they didn't rig their bid with competitors, any lobbying followed the law, and they disclose any family or business ties to council or staff.
  • Once a contract is signed, the director general could approve extra costs of up to 10% of the contract, to a maximum of $50,000, without going back to council.
  • Fines were $1,000 for a person and $2,000 for a company, doubled for a repeat offence, and applied to only four articles.
  • Buying Québec: the 2021 amendment required the town to favour Québec businesses from June 2021 to June 2024. Nothing in the bylaw says what applied after that.

Why it's changing now

Québec adopted a new law on municipal contracts, the Loi sur les contrats des organismes municipaux. According to the draft's preamble, it came into force on April 1, 2026, and requires every municipality to adopt a new contract-management bylaw. Municipalities across Québec have been tabling new bylaws since. Ormstown's draft, Bylaw 129-2026, was announced (notice of motion) and tabled at the October 6, 2026 council sitting, where copies were made available to the public. Once adopted, it would replace both 129-2020 and 129-2021.

What would change, and what each change is meant to do

1. Fewer contracts handed out without competition. A direct award would be allowed only for jobs under $25,000 [art. 12.4]. From $25,000 up to the public-tender threshold, the town would have to send a written invitation to at least two businesses and give them at least 8 days to respond [art. 12.3]. What it does: the mid-sized contracts that could go straight to one supplier under the 2020 rules would now need at least two competing bids.

2. Price comparison for small jobs. For jobs from $5,000 to under $25,000, the town would first need at least two prices and award the work to the lowest. It could go with a single price if it can't get two, or for a “sound administration” reason, as long as it writes down why [art. 12.4]. These contracts don't need a separate council vote if the official has spending authority and the money is in the budget. What it does: brings a basic quote comparison to everyday purchases that had no set method.

3. Thinking before buying. Before any contract, the town must make a “serious assessment” of what it actually needs. It must write that assessment down for contracts of $25,000 or more, using a form attached to the bylaw. It also cannot split one job into smaller pieces to get under a threshold [art. 3.1, 5]. What it does: aims to make sure the town buys the right thing at the right size, and closes the “cut it into pieces” loophole.

4. A firmer nudge to spread the work around. For contracts under $25,000, the town keeps favouring rotation among suppliers “if possible”. It adds a guideline to avoid giving the same business more than three consecutive contracts of the same kind in one year, “as far as possible” [art. 12.6]. Having a business in Ormstown is one of the factors it weighs. What it does: gives more businesses a chance at town work, though the wording leaves the town room to make exceptions.

5. Buying Québec, then Canadian. Below the public-tender threshold, the town would favour Québec businesses and products “when the market allows”, then Canadian ones [art. 13]. In tenders, it could give Québec or Canadian bids, or more sustainable ones, a head start of up to 10% when comparing prices. The winner is still paid its actual bid [art. 14]. A new section also asks the town to consider durability, recycling and environmental impact in what it buys [art. 16].

6. French-language rules. Every contract and every bidding process must respect Québec's Charter of the French Language, and the town may ask bidders for proof [art. 3.3]. This is new to the bylaw.

7. Less room to approve extra costs without council. Once a contract is signed, the director general could approve changes or cost overruns of under 10% of the contract, now up to $25,000 instead of $50,000. Anything larger goes back to council for a vote [art. 11]. What it does: brings bigger mid-contract increases back before elected council members.

8. Much stiffer penalties. The sworn statements and integrity rules stay. The fines change completely [art. 15.4]:

Lying on a bid or sworn statement, or during the contract$5,000–$30,000$15,000–$100,000

Billing the town for money a business isn't owed$5,000–$30,000$15,000–$100,000

Any other breach of the bylaw that counts as an offence$5,000–$30,000$15,000–$100,000

Fines double for a repeat offence, and anyone who helps or encourages the offence is treated the same way. A business that breaks the rules can also lose its contract and be kept off the town's supplier list for up to five years. Employees face discipline up to dismissal.

What stays the same

The director general is still in charge of applying the bylaw. Bidders still sign sworn statements, and gifts to staff or council are still banned. A family or business link to a bidder still doesn't automatically disqualify a bid; the town decides case by case. The director general also continues to choose which businesses are invited to bid and who sits on selection committees, whose names stay confidential.

What this means for you

  • If you run a local business that works for the town, a job worth $25,000 or more would come through a written invitation alongside at least one competitor, not a direct call. For jobs from $5,000, expect to be asked for a quote that will be compared with someone else's.
  • If you bid, you sign a sworn statement. The consequences of getting it wrong would be far more serious.
  • If you're a taxpayer, these are the rules that decide how your money is spent on outside work. You can read the draft and ask council about it before the vote.

How to have your say

The draft is not final. Council will vote on it at a later sitting, and must say then what has changed since the draft. Every regular sitting has two question periods. You can also send a written question to greffe@ormstown.ca by noon on the Friday before the sitting. See our explainer on how bylaws are made for every step.

What we don't know yet

Some details aren't in the draft. The Observer will report them as they become known:

  • The dollar threshold for a public tender. The draft refers to provincial rules but doesn't give the figure that applies to Ormstown.
  • Where the new fines come from. They are far higher than the current bylaw's. It isn't stated whether they are set by the new provincial law or by the town, or who would bring a case.
  • Where the reasons are kept when the town goes with a single price instead of two, and whether residents can see them.
  • Whether council will get a regular list of contracts under $25,000 and of the extra costs the director general approves.
  • What proof of French-language compliance bidders will be asked to provide.
  • The deadline for staff to declare links with a bidder. The draft says “in the days following” the opening of bids.
  • How much changes in practice: how many recent contracts between $25,000 and the tender threshold were awarded directly.
  • When council will vote, and whether the draft will be posted on the town's website.
