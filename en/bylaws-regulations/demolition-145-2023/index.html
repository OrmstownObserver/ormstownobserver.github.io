Construction & Permits · Bylaw 145-2023

Want to tear down a building in Ormstown? A committee of three council members decides, and anyone can object

Bylaw 145-2023, in force since August 31, 2023, bans demolishing a building without the approval of a demolition committee made up of council members. The application is demanding, the public gets ten days to object, and fines start at $10,000. The list of protected heritage buildings it relies on was published blank.

The Ormstown Observer · Bylaws & Regulations Explainer

Key takeaway

  • Demolishing a building, in whole or in part, needs the approval of a demolition committee before the town will issue a certificate (art. 4.1–4.2). Under the bylaw, moving a building also counts as demolition (art. 1.10).
  • The committee is three council members. Since June 1, 2026 they are Mayor Philippe Besombes and councillors René Gauthier and Robert Greig, with Michel-André Émond as substitute. Its decisions are binding and its sittings are public (art. 3.1–3.2).
  • Every application is posted on the building and published as a public notice. Anyone has 10 days to file a written objection with the municipal clerk (art. 4.9, 4.11).
  • After a decision, anyone can ask council to review it within 30 days, and no certificate can be issued until that window closes (art. 4.18, 4.24).
  • Demolishing without approval costs $10,000 to $250,000, and the owner can be ordered to rebuild what was torn down (art. 2.6, 2.8).
  • Annex A, the inventory of heritage buildings the bylaw refers to, is published as a single line: “en attente de la MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent” (awaiting the MRC).

Why this bylaw exists

In March 2021 Québec amended its Cultural Heritage Act and its land-use planning law (Loi sur l'aménagement et l'urbanisme) to give municipalities a stronger hand over demolitions, heritage buildings and building upkeep. Bylaw 145-2023 is Ormstown's response. Its preamble says council wants to regulate the demolition of heritage buildings, and calls the bylaw “an instrument of choice” for protecting built heritage and making sure the land left behind is reused properly.

Notice of motion was given March 6, 2023. A public consultation was held May 17, the bylaw was adopted June 5, a certificate of conformity was issued August 17, and it took effect August 31, 2023. It was moved by Thomas Vandor and seconded by Jacques Guilbault, and passed unanimously.

What counts as a demolition, and which buildings it covers

The bylaw defines demolition broadly: the dismantling, moving, or complete or partial destruction of a building (art. 1.10). Article 4.1 then forbids demolishing any building, in whole or in part, unless the owner has been authorized under the bylaw and holds a certificate.

The text isn't fully consistent about scope. Article 4.2, which creates the obligation to get a certificate, and article 2.6, which sets the fines, both speak of a principal building (immeuble principal), meaning the house or main structure on a lot rather than a shed or detached garage. The municipality's fee schedule points the same way: it lists a committee study fee only for heritage and principal buildings, and a separate $50 certificate for demolishing an accessory building. The likely reading is that sheds and garages go through ordinary permitting, but the bylaw doesn't spell that out (see the open questions below).

Three situations are exempt (art. 4.1):

  • a building that has lost more than half its assessed value to a fire or other disaster;
  • a demolition ordered under articles 227, 229 or 231 of the Loi sur l'aménagement et l'urbanisme;
  • a building threatened by an imminent disaster under Québec's civil-protection law.

What an owner has to file

The application (art. 4.3) is much more than a form. Among other things, the owner must supply:

  • proof of ownership (or of an option to buy), and the names of the contractor, engineer and architect;
  • a detailed description of the building's condition, with interior and exterior photos, and photos of the neighbouring lots;
  • the reasons for demolishing, plus a detailed estimate of what it would cost to restore the building instead;
  • a heritage study of the building prepared by an expert;
  • a preliminary plan for reusing the land left behind (the programme de réutilisation du sol dégagé): schedule and cost, intended use, colour elevations of every façade, a colour rendering of the new building in its setting, a surveyor's site plan, landscaping and parking, and a decontamination schedule if the soil is contaminated;
  • the demolition method and how materials will be disposed of;
  • for any building with rental units, a declaration that each tenant was told in writing, plus the conditions for rehousing them.

An owner who isn't ready to file all of that can first ask the committee for a preliminary opinion on whether the demolition or the reuse plan is acceptable (art. 4.4). An incomplete file is put on hold. If it isn't completed within three months, it lapses and the fee has to be paid again (art. 4.6–4.7).

What it costs

The bylaw leaves fees to the municipal fee schedule. The current one, Bylaw 39-2026 (in force June 1, 2026), sets them as follows. Fees are non-refundable (art. 4.5).

Review of a demolition application: other principal building$250

The municipal fees are the small part. The restoration estimate, heritage study, surveyor's plans and architectural drawings will usually cost far more.

How the process runs, and where the public comes in

  1. Application filedStaff check the file is complete and pass it to the committee. The owner must notify every tenant by registered mail as soon as the application is filed (art. 4.6, 4.8).
  2. Notice posted and publishedAt the owner's expense, a notice is posted on the building where passers-by can see it and published as a public notice. It gives the date, time and place of the committee sitting. For a heritage building, a copy goes to Québec's culture minister (art. 4.9–4.10).
  3. Objections10 daysAnyone can file a written, reasoned objection with the municipal clerk within 10 days of the public notice, or within 10 days of the posting on the building if no notice was published (art. 4.11).
  4. Committee sitting and decisionThe sitting is public. The committee must weigh the building's heritage value, history and condition, the effect on the neighbourhood, the cost of restoration, the proposed reuse of the land, the harm to tenants and to local housing needs, and the objections received. It must give reasons for its decision (art. 4.13–4.14).
  5. Review by council30 daysAnyone can ask council in writing to review the decision. Council can also take it up on its own when the committee has approved a demolition. Council can confirm the decision or substitute its own (art. 4.18–4.20).
  6. MRC veto (heritage buildings only)up to 90 daysIf a heritage building is approved for demolition, the MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent is notified and can overturn (désavouer) the decision within 90 days (art. 4.22–4.23).
  7. Certificate issuedOnly after the 30-day review window closes, and for a heritage building after the MRC either waives its veto or lets the 90 days run out (art. 4.24).

The bylaw also protects tenants. For a building with rental units, someone who wants to buy it and keep it as rental housing can ask the committee for more time to negotiate the purchase. The same applies to a buyer who wants to keep a heritage building's heritage character. The committee can postpone its decision once, by up to two months (art. 4.12).

Conditions and the financial guarantee

When it approves a demolition, the committee can set conditions: deadlines to start and finish both the demolition and the new construction, a reuse plan to be approved, and rehousing terms for tenants (art. 4.15). It can also require a financial guarantee, capped at the building's assessed value, in the form of a certified cheque, a cash deposit, or an irrevocable bank letter of guarantee for at least one year (art. 4.16).

90% of the guarantee comes back once the work done exceeds its value (and, for a new building, once the exterior shell is closed in) and the conditions are met. The last 10% comes back when the reuse plan is fully carried out. If deadlines or conditions are missed, the town can keep the guarantee. The guarantee stays in place if the property is sold, and the new owner must sign on to the same conditions (art. 4.26).

Penalties

  • Demolishing without approval, or in breach of the conditions: $10,000 to $250,000. The maximum rises to $1,140,000 when a company demolishes a building that is legally cited as heritage, or one located on a cited heritage site (art. 2.6).
  • Blocking an inspector, or refusing to show the certificate on site: $300 to $1,000 (art. 2.7).
  • Rebuilding: on top of any fine, the offender must rebuild the building. If they don't, council can have it rebuilt and recover the cost, which becomes a priority claim secured by a legal hypothec on the land (art. 2.8).
  • Each day an offence continues counts as a separate offence (art. 2.10). A certificate can be revoked if work doesn't start or finish on time, if the application contained false information, or if conditions aren't met (art. 2.9).

What this means for residents

If you own a house or other main building and are thinking of taking it down, or taking down a significant part of it, plan for a committee process measured in weeks and a substantial file, not a counter permit. Ask the town for a preliminary opinion before paying for drawings and studies.

If a demolition is proposed near you, watch for the notice posted on the building and the municipality's public notices. The 10-day objection window is short and starts from the publication. An objection has to be in writing, give reasons, and go to the clerk. The committee sitting is public, and anyone, not only neighbours, can ask council to review the decision within 30 days.

Open questions for council and administration

  • Which buildings are “heritage” today? The bylaw defines a heritage building partly by reference to an inventory in Annex A, but the published Annex A reads only “awaiting the MRC du Haut-Saint-Laurent.” Has the MRC's inventory been adopted? Will the annex be updated? Until then, how does the town decide whether an application pays the $300 heritage fee and goes to the MRC for a possible veto?
  • Accessory buildings and partial demolitions. Article 4.1 covers any building, in whole or in part, while articles 4.2 and 2.6 speak only of principal buildings. Does removing a garage, a barn, a porch or a rear addition need committee approval, or only an ordinary permit?
  • Was there a committee before June 2026? The bylaw took effect in August 2023, but the only appointment of its members the Observer has located is resolution 26-06-172, adopted June 1, 2026. How many demolition applications have been filed since 2023, how were they decided, and where are the committee's sitting notices and decisions published?
  • Local heritage council. The bylaw requires the committee to consult a local heritage council on heritage files if the municipality has one (art. 3.3). Does Ormstown have one?
  • A date discrepancy. The cover page says the draft bylaw was tabled and adopted April 3, 2023. The preamble says it was tabled and adopted at the May 1, 2023 sitting. Which is correct?
